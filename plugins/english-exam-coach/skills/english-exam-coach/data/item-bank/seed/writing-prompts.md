# Seed writing prompts (original examples)

All items below are original, written for this plugin as style/format
references for generating new prompts. They are NOT taken from any official
exam or preparation material. Match structure, register and constraints —
never reuse a seed verbatim in a drill; generate a fresh item in its shape.

## IELTS Academic — Task 1 (report, ≥150 words, 20 min)

> The table below shows the percentage of households in three countries that
> owned a bicycle, a car, or both in 2005 and 2025.
>
> | | Bicycle only | Car only | Both |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Country A (2005 → 2025) | 34% → 18% | 22% → 41% | 15% → 24% |
> | Country B (2005 → 2025) | 12% → 15% | 55% → 48% | 20% → 27% |
> | Country C (2005 → 2025) | 41% → 44% | 9% → 13% | 8% → 19% |
>
> Describe the main patterns in the data in your own words, comparing the
> countries and the two years where useful.

*(When a chart cannot be rendered, present the data as a table like this. The
real exam words the rubric slightly differently; this is an original
paraphrase, not the official instruction.)*

## IELTS General Training — Task 1 (letter, ≥150 words, 20 min)

> You recently borrowed a piece of equipment from a neighbour, and it stopped
> working while you were using it. Write a letter to your neighbour. In your
> letter: explain what happened, apologise for the situation, and say what
> you intend to do about it. Begin your letter "Dear ...,".

## IELTS / general — Task 2 (essay, ≥250 words, 40 min)

> Some people believe that public libraries should focus on digital services
> rather than physical books. How far do you agree? Support your view with
> reasons and examples from your own knowledge or experience.

## TOEFL iBT (2026 format) — Write an Email (7 min, three bullet points)

The shape: a situation in two or three sentences; who to write to; **three
bullets**, each a different thing to do (explain, describe, ask). The To and
Subject lines are already filled in, so the learner starts at the greeting.
No word count is set: the standing instruction is to write as much as
possible, in complete sentences. (Instructions here are in our own words.)

> Read the situation and write an email. You have 7 minutes.
>
> You borrowed a projector from the campus media office for a student club
> evening. When you packed it away, you noticed that one of its cables had
> been damaged, and you are not sure whether you will be charged for it.
>
> Write to the media office. Your email should:
> - explain what happened to the cable;
> - describe what you have already done about it;
> - ask what you should do next.
>
> Use full sentences and write as much as you can in the time.
>
> **To:** media.office@northfield-college.example
> **Subject:** Damaged projector cable

## TOEFL iBT (2026 format) — Write for an Academic Discussion (10 min, at least 100 words)

The shape: a professor's post of about 60–80 words that sets up a topic and
ends in a question; two students' posts of about 40–50 words each that take
**different sides**, each with one reason. The learner adds a third voice —
their own opinion, supported, in their own words. A good answer has at least
100 words. (Instructions here are in our own words.)

> Your professor has posted a question for the class, and two students have
> already replied. Add a post of your own. You have 10 minutes.
>
> The class is on urban planning. In your post, give your opinion and
> support it, and add something of your own to the discussion. Aim for at
> least 100 words.
>
> **Professor:** This week we looked at how growing cities deal with traffic.
> Some cities keep widening their roads so that more cars can move through
> them. Others do the opposite: they limit cars in the centre and spend the
> money on buses and trains. Each approach has its supporters. What do you
> think? Which approach serves a growing city better? Why?
>
> **Maya:** I think limiting cars works better. When a city widens a road,
> more people decide to drive, and a few years later the traffic is just as
> slow as before. Good buses and trains can carry far more people in the
> same space.
>
> **Tomas:** I disagree, because many people have no bus or train near their
> home. If a city limits cars before it builds good public transport, those
> people simply cannot get to work. A city should build wider roads first
> and limit cars later.

## B2-level essay (140–190 words)

> In your English class you have been talking about shopping habits. Write an
> essay giving your opinion on the statement: "Small local shops cannot
> survive alongside large online retailers." Include points on **price** and
> **community**, and add one further point of your own.

## C1-level proposal (220–260 words)

> Your college has received a grant to improve student wellbeing and has
> invited proposals. Write a proposal for the college principal outlining one
> or two initiatives, explaining how they would improve wellbeing and why
> they would be a good use of the grant.

## C2-level essay (240–280 words, summarise and evaluate two texts)

> Read the two texts below. Write an essay summarising and evaluating the key
> points from both texts, using your own words throughout.
>
> **Text 1 — The case for slow news.** Rolling coverage rewards speed over
> understanding, and the race to publish first too often means publishing
> before the facts are clear. A daily digest, edited with the benefit of
> hindsight, gives readers a sense of proportion: it separates what genuinely
> mattered from what merely happened to occur that day. Constant alerts, by
> contrast, leave people anxious and strangely uninformed, mistaking the
> volume of updates for real knowledge. Slower, more considered rhythms may
> be the only durable cure for an audience that is exhausted by notifications
> yet no better able to say what is actually going on in the world. *(≈100 words)*
>
> **Text 2 — Speed is a public service.** During emergencies, minutes matter,
> and instant reporting has demonstrably saved lives — warning communities of
> floods, fires, and fast-moving threats while there is still time to act. The
> honest answer to shallow coverage is better editing and clearer sourcing,
> not artificial delay imposed from above. Readers are not children: they can
> weigh a developing story, follow corrections, and choose their own pace
> through the day's events. To slow the news deliberately is to decide on
> their behalf what they are ready to know, and that quiet paternalism is a
> poor trade for the speed a free press can offer. *(≈100 words)*

## TOEFL iBT (2026 format) — Build a Sentence (10 items, about 6 min in all)

Objective task, not band-scored. Score out of 10, **all or nothing per item**:
every tile must sit in the right place, so one misplaced tile scores zero for
that item.

**Every item is TWO sentences.** The learner reads one complete sentence — a
question, a piece of news, a plan — and builds the reply to it. An item with
no first sentence is not this task: the context is what makes one word order
the natural one. Never generate a bare set of tiles.

**Build the set with `coach sentence make` — never lay it out by hand.** You
write the two sentences and mark the tiles; the script prints the frame, the
scrambled tiles and the key, so the blanks always match the tiles.

What the real items look like, and what to write:

- The reply is a row of **5 to 7 blanks**, one per tile. Tiles are lower-case
  words and short phrases (`what time`, `need to`, `in the jacket`).
- A word or two may be **already printed** in the frame — the opening word
  (`The`, `He`), a chunk in the middle (`tell me`, `we can`), or the last
  word. Mark these with `[ ]`.
- The closing full stop or question mark is shown.
- **Most replies are questions**, and the commonest trap is the indirect
  question, where the word order is that of a statement: *Do you know if we
  can…*, *Could you tell me what time we need to…* The rest are statements
  with a relative clause (*The speaker who opened it…*) or a passive (*It was
  made by…*).
- **Two or three items in ten carry one extra tile that is not used** —
  typically the wrong form of a word that is used (`does` beside `do`, `were`
  beside `was`). Never more than one extra tile in an item.

**Keeping the key unique.** After drafting, try to build a *different*
grammatical sentence from the same tiles. The usual culprit is a time or
place phrase that can move (*by Friday*, *in winter*, *instead*): pin it by
printing it in the frame. If two orders really are both right, accept the
second with `--also` rather than marking it wrong.


**Key:**

```text
1. Do you know if we can hand in the report on Monday?   (not used: does)
2. How often do they organise trips in winter?
3. Could you tell me what time we need to leave the hotel?
4. The speaker who opened it was really inspiring.   (not used: were)
5. Have you looked in the jacket that you wore yesterday?
6. What kind of food do they serve there?
7. Is there a printer that students can use for free?
8. It was made by a friend of mine.
9. Do you think we should take the train instead?
10. He promised that it would be fixed by Friday.
```

Mark a learner's answers with `coach sentence check --batch set.json
--responses answers.txt` — one sentence per line, numbered or not. Capitals
and punctuation do not count; the order of the words does.
