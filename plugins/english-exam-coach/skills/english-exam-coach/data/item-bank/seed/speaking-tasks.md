# Seed speaking tasks (original examples)

All items are original, written for this plugin as format references. They
are NOT from any official exam.

## IELTS — Part 2 cue card (1 min prep, speak 1–2 min)

> Describe a tool or object you use almost every day.
> You should say:
> - what it is and how you got it
> - what you use it for
> - how life would change without it
> and explain why it matters more to you than similar objects.

## IELTS — Part 3 follow-up questions (discussion)

> - Do people rely too much on everyday technology? Why or why not?
> - How do the objects people value change as they get older?
> - Should schools teach children to repair things rather than replace them?

## TOEFL iBT (2026 format) — Listen and Repeat (7 sentences, heard once each)

**All seven sentences belong to ONE scenario**, and one speaker says them
all. The scenario line gives the learner a role and says who is speaking; it
is read out once and is not repeated by the learner. There is no preparation
time: each sentence plays once and is repeated straight away.

The sentences **grow, loosely**: the first is one short clause of 5–7 words;
the later ones run 11–14 words and add a list, a condition or a relative
clause. One shorter sentence in the middle is normal. Nothing reaches the
high teens — a sentence that long is harder than the real task.

> **Scenario (read first, not repeated):** "You are starting a part-time job
> at a campus bookshop. Listen to your supervisor and repeat what she says.
> Repeat each sentence once."
>
> 1. Welcome to the campus bookshop. *(5 words)*
> 2. New textbooks are on the shelves by the window. *(9)*
> 3. Used copies are cheaper, and we keep them near the back. *(11)*
> 4. Please ask customers for their student card before you give a discount. *(12)*
> 5. Returns are accepted within two weeks of purchase. *(8)*
> 6. If the card reader stops working, restart it and try the payment again. *(13)*
> 7. Any order that arrives after four o'clock should be unpacked the
>    following morning. *(13)*

*(Other scenarios that work: a first day as a museum guide, a tour of a
sports centre for new members, a safety briefing in a teaching lab, a host
explaining house rules to a new flatmate. Keep the vocabulary everyday; the
difficulty comes from length and structure, not rare words.)*

## TOEFL iBT (2026 format) — Take an Interview (4 questions, no preparation time)

**One interview, ONE everyday topic, opened by a short scenario.** Four
unrelated questions is the wrong shape, and so are four one-line questions:
after the first, **every question comes with a lead-in of one to three
sentences**, so each interviewer turn runs about 35–60 words. The learner
has to hold the lead-in and answer the question at the end of it.

The four turns climb in a fixed order:

1. a **factual question about the learner** (right after the opener);
2. their **own reaction or preference, and why** — set up by two contrasting
   ways people feel;
3. **agree or disagree** with a view the interviewer states and illustrates;
4. their **opinion on a policy or proposal**, usually ending "Why or why not?"

> **Scenario (read first):** "You are taking part in a study about how
> people use public libraries. A researcher will talk to you online and ask
> you some questions."
>
> 1. **Interviewer:** Thank you for joining me today. I'm studying how people
>    use libraries now that so much reading has moved online, and I'd like to
>    ask you a few questions. First, how often do you go to a library, and
>    what do you usually do there? *(45 words)*
> 2. **Interviewer:** I see. People feel differently about studying in public
>    places. Some find that a quiet reading room helps them concentrate.
>    Others say they work better at home, where nobody is watching. Which is
>    closer to your own experience, and why do you think that is? *(45 words)*
> 3. **Interviewer:** Thanks. Now I'd like your opinion. Some people argue
>    that libraries no longer need printed books, because almost everything
>    can be read on a screen. They say the space would be better used for
>    computers and group study rooms. Do you agree that libraries should
>    replace most of their printed books? Why or why not? *(55 words)*
> 4. **Interviewer:** That's helpful. I have one last question. Many towns
>    have to decide how to spend limited public money, and libraries are
>    expensive to run. Do you think local governments should keep paying for
>    libraries even when fewer people visit them? Why or why not? *(44 words)*

*(**Volume of an answer:** the answer clock is commonly reported as about
45 seconds, which is roughly 90–130 words at a natural pace — five to eight
sentences. Answers far short of that read as underdeveloped; racing past the
clock gets cut off mid-sentence, which costs more than stopping early. Other
topics that work: commuting, eating habits, part-time work, learning online,
city parks, using a phone.)*

## B2/C1-level collaborative task (paired discussion, ~3 min)

> Your town wants to make its centre more attractive to young families.
> Here are some ideas being considered:
> **car-free Sundays · a toy library · later shop hours · a splash park ·
> free parking for parents**
> Talk to each other about how effective each idea might be, then decide
> which TWO the town should try first.

## C2-level long turn (2 min from a question card)

> "Convenience has quietly replaced quality as the thing we optimise for."
> How far do you agree? You may consider: food, communication, travel.
